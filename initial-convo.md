A few things that get interesting fast:
The model clarifies the double audience problem. Writers submit to communities (like subreddits) run by editor-moderators who maintain a taste/aesthetic. Readers follow communities they trust. The editor is the missing piece Reddit lacks for literary content — someone with actual judgment, not just a mod with ban powers.

Submittable/Duotrope: writer tools only, no reader experience

Wattpad: reader-facing but popularity-driven, YA-heavy
Lit mags online: siloed, no cross-community discovery

And that's actually the harder problem.
The tech is essentially Reddit with a submission queue — not trivial to build, but well-understood. The real work is:
Recruiting editors who are simultaneously good readers, willing to do unpaid (or low-paid) labor, consistent, and not going to burn out or go rogue after six months. That's a rare profile. Literary community is full of people who want to be editors but fewer who will actually show up every week.
Cold start. You need readers to attract writers, writers to attract readers, and editors to attract both. All three have to bootstrap together, which means the founder is essentially running a lit mag for the first year just to prove the concept works in one community before it scales.
Quality variance across communities. Once you open it up, some communities will be well-run and some will be dead or low-quality. That drags the platform's reputation down even if the good ones are excellent.
The comparison I'd make is less Reddit and more early Electric Literature or The Rumpus — those worked because a small group of people with genuine taste committed hard to a specific vision before worrying about scale.
So the real question might be: do you want to build this platform, or do you want to run one community really well first and see if the platform case emerges from that?
The second path is lower risk and tells you faster whether the editorial model actually works.
